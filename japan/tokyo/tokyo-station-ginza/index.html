Tokyo Station & Ginza

Tokyo Station’s red-brick Marunouchi face looks toward the Imperial Palace, while the Yaesu side opens toward Shinkansen platforms, bus facilities and underground retail. From the station, Yurakucho leads into Ginza, whose name recalls the Edo-period silver mint once located here.

A station with two fronts

Pick the side before you leave the platform

The historic Marunouchi facade, office towers and palace moat occupy the west side; Yaesu is the practical side for many Shinkansen transfers, highway buses and Daimaru. Rail concourses and shopping levels overlap, so follow your named operator and gate before choosing a street exit.

West

Marunouchi

Marunouchi is the west facade: step outside to compare the restored brick station block with the newer glass towers, then continue along Naka-dori toward the palace moat. The East Gardens are beyond their own entrance and calendar, so keep the street walk as the reliable route.

Use for · palace and facade
East

Yaesu

Yaesu faces the Shinkansen side and the bus terminals, with Daimaru and underground streets close to the exits. Use this side when you need a coach, an eastbound connection or an ekiben; it is not the side shown in the classic station-front photographs.

Use for · buses and east side
Inside

Gransta and transfer levels

Gransta includes shops both inside and outside JR’s ticket gates, and the long platform corridors can make a quick purchase take longer than expected. Tokyo Ramen Street and Yaesu retail floors are outside the same paid-area decision; check before tapping out if you are transferring.

Use for · planned shopping
South

Yurakucho connection

Walk south from Marunouchi through the office district or follow the rail viaduct toward Yurakucho, then continue to Ginza. Yurakucho is the useful connection when you want to leave the station without walking back through its underground corridors.

With luggage

Use a defined storage plan

Coin lockers can fill. Compare hotel storage and staffed services, then save closing time.

Before departure

Rehearse the return

Know whether a final purchase is inside the gates and allow time for the long platform walk.

Do not photograph a locker and assume that is enough

Save the station level, nearby shop or gate, locker number and closing/access conditions. A giant station can contain many identical corridors.

A central Tokyo line

Red brick, garden edge, then blue hour

Make the palace gardens optional, not the center of the route: the Imperial Household Agency posts the East Gardens’ current opening days and hours. If the date does not work, Naka-dori, the station facade, Kitte or Nihombashi still make a coherent walk.

CENTRAL TOKYO WALK

Marunouchi → palace edge → Yurakucho → Ginza

4–8 hours
Start

Marunouchi facade

Begin in front of the Marunouchi facade and look back from the plaza to see how the brick station sits against the high-rise district. If you want to enter the East Gardens, check the current gate and date before leaving the station area.

Daylight

Garden or architecture

Follow Naka-dori’s tree-lined blocks for the contemporary Marunouchi streetscape, or detour to the East Gardens only if the official calendar shows the entrance open. The garden is a separate visit, not a shortcut to Ginza.

Late afternoon

Yurakucho

Continue toward Yurakucho, where the rail arches and small dining streets change the scale from Marunouchi’s office towers. Stop here for a casual meal or coffee before the department stores and wider avenues of Ginza.

Blue hour

Ginza

Enter Ginza from Yurakucho and choose one focus: window-shop Chuo-dori, visit Kabukiza’s gallery or program, or reserve a counter meal. The main avenue becomes pedestrian-only on some weekend and holiday periods; verify the current schedule before planning around it.

Calendar-aware planning

Never hard-code the palace garden into Monday

East Gardens access follows the Imperial Household Agency’s date-specific notices and can be affected by closures. Check the calendar before you walk over; when it is closed, stay in Marunouchi or continue toward Nihombashi and Ginza without losing the day.

  • Read the Imperial Household Agency’s East Gardens notice for the exact date, gate and hours.
  • Treat the garden as a planned stop with time for its entrance and walk, rather than a shortcut between station districts.
  • If the official calendar does not fit, use Marunouchi Naka-dori, Kitte or the Nihombashi streets as the walk instead.
  • The red-brick facade and Ginza avenue remain accessible street walks when the garden entrance is closed.

Quick answers

Tokyo Station and Ginza FAQ

What is the difference between Marunouchi and Yaesu?

Marunouchi is the west-facing brick facade and palace side. Yaesu is the Shinkansen, bus-terminal and Daimaru side; confirm the exact line and exit before following a long indoor connection.

Can I walk from Tokyo Station to Ginza?

Yes. Follow the Marunouchi district south to Yurakucho and then Ginza; Tokyo’s official guide places Ginza within walking distance of the station. Use a train when carrying luggage, in heavy rain or when your destination is at Higashi-Ginza.

Is Tokyo Station a good hotel base?

Choose the station area for rail changes, station shopping and a polished Marunouchi evening; choose Ginza for department-store food halls, galleries and kabuki. For more casual late dining, compare Shinjuku or Shibuya before booking a hotel.

Are the East Gardens open daily?

No. The East Gardens use a date-specific calendar with closure days and occasional restrictions; check the Imperial Household Agency notice for your exact visit date.
